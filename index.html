What Happened

In the spring of 1914, a travelling showman vanished from a Marseille boarding house, leaving behind an unpaid bill, a wooden camera and a notebook. For years before that he had toured Europe with exhibitions of moving pictures; wherever he played, audiences rioted, wept, or walked into rivers. The camera and the notebook drifted east with the war's debris and ended up in the back room of a Cairo antiquities dealer.

That is where Nathaniel Cross found them in 1923 — or believes he did. Cross is a self-made man of the movies — nickelodeons, then features, now vice-president of production at Imperial Pictures — and for thirty years he has been losing a war with his own body: disseminated sclerosis, sanatoria, radium water, mediums, and since 1923 a wheelchair. He was desperate enough to listen to a Pasadena occultist everyone else laughed at, Dr. Ignatius Pell, who had been hunting the camera for ten years and needed a rich man to fetch it; it was Pell who suggested Cairo for the location scouting. The camera's brass plate read Exhibitions of the Last Sunset. The notebook, Le Masque du Dernier Couchant, in Pell's translation, promised Cross what his doctors could not: that whoever stages the Masque, and lets enough eyes see it, will take a seat “in the dark theatre that has no last reel.”

The rite is a sexual one. It needs masked celebrants, a west-facing room at sunset, and a vessel — a young woman who says yes, however that yes is obtained, without knowing what she is really giving. So Cross built the Silver Circle: rich and powerful men — producers, a senator, a bandleader — and the aspiring actresses that Imperial's studio cars deliver to them, promised screen tests and parts. Robes and Latin make it a secret society; cameras behind a curtain give Cross a hold over every man there. To the young women it is one more thing they are told is normal. Two earlier vessels fainted, then recovered. Cross needed a stronger one.

Six weeks ago, Vivienne Ashcroft — a fading silent star who now manages young talent — got her best client a screen test for Queen of the Nile: Evelyn Vale, 22, from Dayton, Ohio, warm, ambitious and two small parts into a career. Evelyn tested in a gold mask from the costume department and didn't get the part. But Cross and Pell watched the test footage, and Pell had found his vessel. Soon a studio Lincoln, driven by Earl Hooper, was calling at Evelyn's bungalow: parties, people who matter, “a part in my next picture” — and leave your agent out of it; agents only take ten per cent. Vivienne knew none of it.

On Saturday, June 5, 1926, at sunset, Evelyn was led into the west salon wearing nothing but the gold mask from her screen test. Cameraman Arthur Bell, hired privately and paid by Cross himself, cranked the showman's camera. As the sun touched the sea, Evelyn went limp among the masked men — and through the warm, dark lens Bell watched her still there, among the masks, while her body lay slack on the cushions. The camera had opened a window, and her mind had gone through it. Bell didn't understand what he had seen — only that it was wrong. In his panic he wrenched the camera open; the last roll tore and crumpled in the gate. He fled.

The next day he went to the only honest man he knew outside the studio: Jack Mercer, the ex-cop turned private eye who, in 1924, had refused the studio's money over a set accident. Mercer smelled fear, took it for gin, and showed him the door; Bell left his card on the desk. On Tuesday night, on Cross's order, his hired muscle Frank Dugan, an ex-deputy, ran Bell's roadster off Mulholland Drive, and Bell's house was quietly “burgled” the same night. A Police Commissioner on Cross's payroll closed the case by morning. Mercer never saw the three-line item in the papers.

Evelyn's body — breathing, unresponsive — was driven by Hooper that same night to Blackwater Lodge, Cross's retreat in the San Bernardino Mountains, where Nurse Whitcomb keeps it alive and Dugan keeps everyone else away. Cross does not fear her rescue. He fears her death: as long as Evelyn's body lives, every complete print of the film carries her, and whoever watches it to the end is pulled into her endless sunset orgy — and loses every restraint they had. If she dies, the film becomes only film.

There are two films. The one the censors passed is “Sunset Serenade”, a harmless masquerade of veils and a waltz, shot on a sound stage; it will run before every showing of Imperial's million-dollar epic Queen of the Nile, and Kalb, Roth and the exchanges know nothing else. The other is Bell's raw footage of the Masque — and the rite demands it whole, every frame as the camera kept it. At the studio lab, negative cutter Ruth Abernathy spent three nights on it: the last roll torn and crumpled where Bell had wrenched the camera open, and Pell demanding that not one frame be lost. She flattened, patched and re-perforated the damaged frames by hand under a magnifier, then inspected all twelve thousand frames of the reel, one by one, her job “and more” at stake. She never saw the film run — she saw all of it, frozen, one frame at a time; she is in a rest home now, painting sunsets on the wall with her fingers. Until Saturday night the raw negative and the work print stay at the mansion; after the Masque Pell carries the negative to the lab, where the night shift prints forty copies of a vice-president's “private test” without projecting a foot of it. On Sunday afternoon he and Hooper swap them into the Queen of the Nile cases. At 10 pm the cases leave Central Station by express for film exchanges across the country. On Friday, June 25, the lights go down in forty cinemas.

On Thursday, Evelyn's brother Thomas arrives from Ohio. Nobody answers at her bungalow; at the police station a desk sergeant tells him the studio has already called — she's on vacation, the family has been informed. That evening, at the dark bungalow, he meets Vivienne, who has been calling Evelyn for two weeks. On Friday morning, June 18, she takes him to the one honest detective she knows. Mercer looks at the photograph — and at the card Bell left on his desk — and calls the reporter he trades favours with, Billy Mulligan.

Evelyn cannot be saved. The investigators can only decide what her ending will be — and how many strangers share it.

01 · At a Glance · One-shot, 4–5 hours

The Core

Cross is visibly the villain. His plan is the mystery. Find Evelyn Vale; learn why cameraman Arthur Bell was killed; uncover the cursed short hidden in a nationwide release — and face the fact that Evelyn cannot be saved.

Three Supernatural Facts

The Camera — Cross's eldritch camera opened a window at a masked rite, and Evelyn's mind went through. Her body lingers in a coma; every complete print looks onto where she is (see The Showman & Carcosa).

The Film — Anyone who watches a complete print of the real reel, uninterrupted, is drawn into the endless orgy of her sunset: erotic fixation, loss of all inhibition, violent obsession, psychic collapse. Every complete copy is lethal while Evelyn's body lives.

The Ending — Only two things break it: Evelyn dies, or every copy is destroyed. Nothing brings her back.

Five Revelations — any sensible order

1 · Private ritesBell filmed the Silver Circle's masked rites for Cross. → 01, 04

The Objective

Before Sunday 10 pm: destroy the raw negative, the 40 real prints and Cross's work print. The censors' decoys don't count.

After the train leaves: only Evelyn's death stops opening night.
Cross's death alone stops nothing — Dr. Pell carries on.

GM Rules

  • Leads, not locks. A failed move adds trouble, cost or time — never a dead end.
  • The world moves. Cross reacts to leverage, not curiosity; the Red Clock runs on the calendar.
  • Fair play. No mandatory clue order or roll; if players guess early, let them act.
  • On a 6−, make a GM move: reveal an unwelcome truth, put someone on the spot, cost them resources, advance a clock.

Safety & Boundaries

Theme: the sexual exploitation of young women in Hollywood. Name it in Lines & Veils.

Never depicted: sexual acts or abuse — only what PCs find out and the aftermath. PCs are witnesses, never participants.

Off-screen: the screenings. Ask first how a mercy killing is handled.

02 · GM Only · The Truth

Cross, the Camera & the Rite

Where the Camera Came From

In 1923, scouting Cairo for an Egyptian picture, Nathaniel Cross bought a wooden camera from an antiquities dealer near the Khan el-Khalili — the dealer Pell had spent ten years looking for. The dealer said it had belonged to a travelling showman who toured Europe before the Great War with “exhibitions” of moving pictures — exhibitions after which audiences rioted, wept or walked into rivers. The showman vanished in 1914.

The camera looks like a 1910s hand-crank model, but its lens is ground from a dark, faintly warm glass, and its brass plate reads, in French, Exhibitions of the Last Sunset. Overcome Fear serious, if studied closely.

How Cross Knows the Rite

With the camera came the showman's leather notebook, “Le Masque du Dernier Couchant”. Occultist Dr. Ignatius Pell translated it for Cross. It prescribes:

  • masked celebrants and a sexual rite in a west-facing room, filmed at the moment of sunset;
  • a vessel at its centre whose “yes” is required — however it is obtained — and who does not know what she is for;
  • afterwards, prints struck from the negative — whole, “not a frame lost” — “so the sunset may be shared.”

The Silver Circle provides the celebrants: powerful men, and the young actresses Imperial's cars deliver to them. Two earlier vessels, Lila Marsh and Betty Crane, fainted and recovered, remembering only “a long sunset”; Bentham paid them off. Evelyn was picked by Pell from her Queen of the Nile screen test — in a gold mask from the costume department — and courted by Cross behind her agent's back. She did not recover.

Why Cross Does It

Public face: a liberal aesthete who says taboo imprisons humanity. In fact a predator who has used his studio for years to feed powerful men young women — the films are his hold over them.

Real motive: Pell's translation promises that when enough eyes have seen the sunset, the Showman returns — and the one who staged the Masque will take a seat “in the dark theatre that has no last reel.” Cross is 58; disseminated sclerosis has kept him in a wheelchair since 1923, and the next years mean blindness, paralysis and a slow drowning in his own lungs. He doesn't fear death; he fears being locked in. A seat where the body doesn't matter is everything to him. What really waits: The Showman & Carcosa.

The Plan

The shortThe decoy: “Sunset Serenade”, an 11-minute masquerade of veils and a waltz, shot on a sound stage and passed by the censors. The real reel: Bell's raw Masque footage, uncut — every frame. Seen complete, it shows the viewer the whole orgy, and draws them in.
The vehicleAttached as the programme short to every print of Imperial's summer epic “Queen of the Nile”. Exchanges and cinemas unpack both together.

The deadlineSunday 4–6 pm Pell and Hooper swap the real cans into the cases at the lab; 10 pm the Southern Pacific express. Opening night: next Friday, nationwide.
The anchorEvelyn's body at Blackwater Lodge, kept alive by Nurse Whitcomb. Cross needs her breathing for months.
His fearNot her rescue — her death. A hospital might lose her; a grieving brother might grant mercy. So: guards, secrecy, a nurse who asks no questions.
His successorIf Cross dies, Pell sees the shipment through.

The Showman & Carcosa

The players will never need any of this. It is here so the Keeper knows what is behind the curtain — and can let it show at the edges. Never name it at the table.

The Play

The King in Yellow is a play in two acts. The first is harmless. The second destroys whoever reads it. The first act ends at a masquerade in Carcosa, on the shore of Lake Hali, where at the hour the twin suns set behind the lake every guest must unmask. One guest, the Stranger, wears a pale mask — and it is no mask. “You, sir, should unmask.” — “Indeed?” — “I wear no mask.” What follows is the second act.

The Showman

A French impresario who read the play in the 1890s and never came back from the second act. Since then he has been the King's showman — the Stranger's servant on this side. He wants the play performed, and the moving picture is the perfect stage: a book must be read, a film is seen, by hundreds at once, without anyone agreeing to it. His exhibitions before the war were rehearsals. Whoever saw one complete sat, for a moment, at the masquerade — and came back to riot, weep or walk into the river.

In 1914 he vanished from Marseille. He did not lose the camera; he left it behind for the next impresario. Le Masque du Dernier Couchant is his staging script: how to film the masquerade of the first act so that the second begins in the auditorium.

The Camera & the Rite

The lens is ground from dark, warm glass from the shore of Lake Hali. It does not record; it looks through. The rite needs a west-facing room at the exact moment of sunset because the twin suns of Carcosa set behind the lake at the same moment — the two sunsets overlap, and the window opens. The “figures that weren't the guests” Bell saw are the court of Carcosa. Pell had the gold mask from Evelyn's screen test engraved with a sign from the notebook: a twisted golden glyph, the Yellow Sign.

Evelyn & the Windows

Evelyn's mind is not “on the film”. It is at the masquerade, in the role of Cassilda. Every complete copy is a window onto it; whoever watches to the end climbs through — and in Carcosa everyone unmasks, which is why viewers lose all restraint. Her living body holds her in the role and keeps the windows open.

MercyHer body dies: the masquerade loses its Cassilda. She alone may take off her mask and leave. Every window shows an empty room — just film.
AshesEvery copy destroyed, the body lives: the windows close, but she stays at the masquerade — no audience, no way out, alone with the court — until the body dies a year later. A year in Carcosa may be longer than a year.

The clean, heroic ending condemns her; the darkest act is the only mercy. Only Pell knows — his journal, or Pell himself under pressure. If the players never find the journal, it reaches Mercer by mail a month later.

Pell, the Believer

1913, Marseille: a young American student sees one of the Showman's last exhibitions. The hall riots; people wade into the harbour. Pell stays in his seat and weeps with happiness: he has seen the masquerade and come back. For ten years he hunts the Showman; in a Paris bookseller's back room he reads Act I and recognises the masquerade. He has never dared the second act — his one fear, greater than his fear of Cross.

1923: he learns the camera is with a Cairo dealer. He has no money, no studio, no cinemas — so he finds a man who has all three and nothing left to lose: Cross, thirty years into his illness, newly in a wheelchair. Cross believes he found the camera. Pell led him to it. The “seat in the dark theatre” is Pell's translation, chosen for a dying man. What Pell wants: when enough eyes have seen the masquerade, the Showman returns — and names Pell his successor. In Carcosa, Cross will be one more spectator, unmasked.

At the Table — Never Named

  • Cassilda's Song is the waltz Evelyn hums; Ruth sings it between fits: “Along the shore the cloud waves break, / The twin suns sink behind the lake, / The shadows lengthen / In Carcosa.”
  • The Yellow Sign on the gold mask, on the lab still, in the Masque footage.
  • At dusk in Evelyn's room the shadows fall the wrong way.
  • Pell's journal is a believer's diary, not a log.
  • Whether the Showman ever returns is the Keeper's to decide. The notebook promises it; Chambers would say the King doesn't need him.

Rules of the Curse

Anchor — While Evelyn's body lives, every complete copy is potent. Her death makes all prints mundane instantly.

Negatives — Frames on a light table count as stills (moderate); a negative can't be “watched”. Complete & uninterrupted — The rite must be seen whole, every frame as the camera kept it. A copy missing a single frame (any ordinary splice), a broken projection or a reel stopped midway is harmless; the decoy is just a film. (A PC who wants to know what's on the real reel: offer a few interrupted seconds, Overcome Fear serious.) Fragments and stills are unsettling, not lethal: Overcome Fear moderate. Nights of handling them, as Ruth did, wear a mind away piece by piece. Each time a still is viewed, Evelyn's face has turned a little further toward the camera.

Full viewing — Overcome Fear is not allowed: a permanent critical mental disorder (erotic fixation, compulsion to return to the sunset, catatonia). For a theatre audience: inhibitions collapse, then violence, fires, suicides — always off-screen.

Destroy every copy — When the last complete copy burns (nitrate burns fast and hot) or loses a frame, the windows close and the curse ends — but Evelyn stays at the masquerade, alone, until her body dies. Her body lives on, empty.

No cure — There is no ritual of return. The notebook says so plainly: “The vessel is spent.”

05 · Scene One

Opening: Mercer's Office

Read aloud Friday morning, Los Angeles, 1926. The sun is already hot on Spring Street, and the Venetian blinds of the office on the fourth floor cut it into bars across a desk buried in unpaid bills, cold coffee — and one engraved business card. A big, sunburned young man in his one good suit stands in the doorway, turning his hat in his hands. Beside him, in furs despite the heat, a woman whose face was on every marquee in 1919. He holds a photograph: a pretty young woman with a film-star smile. "Mr. Mercer? Thomas Vale. Miss Ashcroft says you can't be bought. My sister Evelyn hasn't written home in two weeks. The police say she's on vacation. Evelyn doesn't do that."

Scene Beats

  1. The photograph. Thomas shows Evelyn's picture. Mercer knows the name — the one Bell was raving about. Ask Thomas's player: what did the desk sergeant say when you told him you are the family?
  2. Flashback. Ask Mercer's player to describe Bell in this office two weeks ago. Give them Bell's words: "I looked through the lens and she was still moving, Mercer. Still with them. And she was lying right there on the cushions. Both. I don't know what I saw. Get that kid out of that house. Evelyn Vale. Please." Bell's card is still on the desk.
  3. The agent. Ask Vivienne's player: what did Evelyn tell you on your last call? (Mr. Cross “had plans for her”.)
  4. The reporter. Mercer calls Billy: “Bring your notebook.” Billy arrives within the hour and works Mercer's phone (his playbook: What Your Calls Turn Up).
  5. The card. When someone dials Bell's number, Eddie Kaplan answers the camera-department phone. A pause. “Arthur's dead. Car accident, last week.” Then, quietly: “Not on the phone. Studio gate, five o'clock.”
  6. Pressure, later. Once the PCs have asked around about Bell, Malloy calls Mercer in the evening — a friendly warning, and an invitation.

Search for Something +INT

Search an area or situation for an item or information. 10+ you find something useful. 7–9 you find it, choose 1: of limited use · you or an ally in danger · lose an opportunity · discover something frightening (moderate) · expend resources.

Consult Knowledge +INT

Draw on your knowledge. 10+ the GM tells you something interesting and useful. 7–9 interesting, but maybe not useful. The GM may ask, “how do you know this?”

Detect Truth +EMP

Observe or converse to learn the truth. 10+ ask 2. 7–9 ask 1, and suffer a setback or lose an NPC's trust: Who is in control here? What does someone really want? How does someone feel about my character? What happened here earlier? What is likely to happen next?

Convince +EMP

Control an NPC's actions or opinions when you have leverage. 10+ they do it if you promise to remove the leverage. 7–9 only after you prove the leverage is gone or do a task for them. Already willing? No roll.

Defy Danger MGT · SPD · WIL · EMP · INT

Aid or Interfere +CON

Before another player rolls. CON = your connections with them. 10+ turn their 6− into 7–9 or 7–9 into 10+ (or the reverse). 7–9 you're exposed to danger; only 7–9 → 10+ (or 10+ → 7–9).

Attack up Close +MGT

10+ you injure the enemy; you may raise the injury one level and take one yourself. 7–9 you injure it, choose 1: it injures you · lose a valuable resource · danger increases.

Attack at a Distance +SPD

10+ you injure the enemy. 7–9 you injure it, choose 1: injury one level lower · lose ammunition or a resource · take damage from the enemy or its allies.

Overcome Fear +WIL

Faced with frightening creatures, situations or revelations. 10+ resist this and similar fears for the rest of the session. 7–9 resist for this encounter, choose 1: a mental disorder one level below the threat · a moderate injury · danger increases · you run away mindlessly · lose a resource. 6− the GM may give a disorder at the full threat level.

The Mercy Question · how to run it

Once clue 08 is known, someone will think it. Don't rush past that moment — it is the scenario's real climax.

  • Put someone on the spot: it should be a choice made at the bedside, ideally with Thomas present or deliberately kept away.
  • No roll to do it to a helpless body — but whoever does it must Overcome Fear (serious); Thomas, if he learns of it unasked, must too.
  • Obstacles: Dugan's orders (deadly force), Whitcomb (“not while I'm on duty” — unless turned), Thomas himself.
  • Show it worked: at that moment Ruth falls still, the lab stills fade to grey, Cross, in Bel Air, takes off his dark glasses and looks into the light — for one moment it doesn't hurt.
  • Never the only road before Sunday 10 pm: burning every copy also works.
  • In a city hospital: a death on the ward is examined. Whitcomb (if turned) can sign “heart failure”. Otherwise the police come — and Bentham buries it, because Cross wants no inquest either.

Blackwater Lodge

Primary fact. Evelyn's body is kept at a small mountain-lake lodge under Whitcomb's care. Cross needs her breathing — her death disarms every print. Dugan's guards stop anyone reaching her. Nothing wakes her. Dugan's green Packard stands by the boathouse.

Approach & Layout

A secluded timber retreat above a small lake in the San Bernardino Mountains.

Motor roadOne narrow road from the highway — the only vehicle route.
Service pathFoot trail to the kitchen; supply van stops here.
BoathouseLakeside; rowboat, cover for a water approach.
VerandaMain entrance; Dugan's post by day. Two chained watchdogs (Thomas: Animal Empathy).
Ground floorKitchen & service rooms; nurse's quarters.
UpstairsEvelyn's bedroom. Medical cot, drip, flowers nobody sent.

Who Does What

Dugan challenges unknown visitors before they reach the house; fires on anyone breaching the perimeter or removing the patient; doesn't want a siege.
Guards (2) watch approaches and deliveries; not fanatics, incomplete information.
Whitcomb can be reached through deliveries or a discreet message; may quietly help once shown Cross is lying. Pell rings her every evening at 7 — if nobody answers properly, Cross knows within the hour (Amber +1).

Approaches

Never require just one.

Recon Follow supply deliveries; time the patrols.

Social Contact Whitcomb; claim medical authority or family rights (Thomas).

Stealth Wooded slope, service path or across the lake — after establishing sight lines.

Negotiate Appeal to the nurse, bribe a guard, legal pressure on Dugan.

Force Outmanoeuvre or confront armed guards. Make the danger plain before guns come out.

At the Bedside

Evelyn breathes; at dusk her eyelids flutter and she hums a waltz; the shadows in the room fall the wrong way. Thomas can hold her hand; nothing happens. A pillow lies on the chair. Put someone on the spot: mercy, rescue, or walk away?

Taking her away (hospital, Thomas's custody) denies Cross his anchor's safety but doesn't break the curse. Killing her does — instantly, everywhere.

If the PCs take Evelyn

Getting out: Dugan fires on anyone removing the patient; Whitcomb refuses a night drive in a motorcar on that road: “She wouldn't survive it. Come back with an ambulance, or with a judge.” With one, she travels with a nurse to a hospital — San Bernardino County or Queen of Angels in LA. Cross: Amber +2 at once. Bentham files a kidnapping complaint (“a studio employee under the studio's medical care”) and Brannigan's men look for the car. The law: Thomas is next of kin; Bentham's unsigned leave form and the ban on doctors sink the studio's claim — with Malloy's help or a lawyer. Doctors: “catatonic stupor”. They can keep her alive for years. The curse goes on; the mercy question follows her to the ward.

14 · Final Act

Cross, the Hitman & the Endings

Play Cross Intelligently

He assumes the PCs can prove nothing and reacts to leverage, not curiosity. Each of his moves exposes a weakness: a legal threat shows which evidence matters; surveillance can be spotted; moving Ruth leaves a trail. When he believes he has won, he tells them the truth — because the truth is the cruellest thing he owns.

Stage Six: Dugan Comes Down

When the PCs hold something Cross can't do without — the negative, the work print, Evelyn — he calls Dugan down from the lodge to take it back. Dugan doesn't negotiate. The lodge, meanwhile, has only two guards.

Ashes

Every can burned. Evelyn is moved to a sanatorium; Thomas reads her letters aloud each Sunday. Every evening at dusk she still hums the waltz. A year later she dies at sunset, “of unclear causes”. Only if the PCs really missed a copy: the same evening, a private screening in a house in Bel Air.

Mercy

A pillow, a pistol, or a door left open on a cold mountain night. Across the country, forty cans of film are just film. Whitcomb says, later, that she looked as if she'd taken off a mask. Someone has to tell Ma and Pa Vale. Pell takes the notebook and is gone by morning — looking for the next Cross; the raw negative, wherever it ended up, is only film now.

Opening Night

Montage: cans unloaded in San Francisco, Portland, Denver, Chicago, Miami, New York. Ushers. A string quartet's waltz. The lights go down. Fade to black on a headline.

Table Content Boundaries

Theme: sexual exploitation in early Hollywood — name it before play. Never depicted: sexual acts or abuse; PCs are never participants. Off-screen: audiences' madness. Ask first: whether a mercy killing is narrated or faded out.
